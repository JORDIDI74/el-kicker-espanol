Catoctin's Foreign Aid

Spanish foreign-exchange student Diaz-Nichols is secret weapon on Cougars.

The Frederick News-Post · Nov. 9, 2023 · By Greg Swatek

THURMONT — On a scale of one to 10, Catoctin football player Ashton Merritt blurts out, without hesitation, "11."

It's an improbable story he will be telling for the rest of his life. And it has nothing to do with the injury-ravaged Cougars (4-6) overcoming the odds and winning their first playoff game since 2019 last week, or even the task ahead Thursday evening in the second round of the 1A North playoffs against top-seeded Patterson Mill in Bel Air.

Even Catoctin coach Mike Rich, the son of a football coach, has never seen anything like it in almost 30 years of being around the sport.

The story centers around Jorge Diaz-Nichols, a junior foreign-exchange student at Catoctin suddenly turned secret weapon for the Cougars' varsity football team as a virtually automatic and strong-legged place kicker.

It begins in late August, right after Diaz-Nichols stepped off the plane in Baltimore from his hometown of Madrid, barely able to speak much English.

Preseason practice was already underway at Catoctin. Rich was watching film of a recent scrimmage with his junior-varsity players when he was summoned to the office of Keith Bruck, the school's athletic director.

Bruck had told Rich that he had a foreign-exchange student in his office who was interested in playing football. And, as Rich made his way there, all he could think was, "OK, well, he has no experience. We'll give him a go and see how it goes, do him a favor."

But then Diaz-Nichols rose out of his office chair to greet Rich, and the coach was stunned. Diaz was 6-foot-4, 210 pounds and appeared to be athletic.

"You can play. You have a spot," Rich promptly told him.

Now, the question was, where do you play him? Diaz-Nichols had never played football before. Having spent his life in Spain, he knew almost nothing about the sport, not even the names of NFL teams or famous players.

"I didn't know that the kicker exist," he said.

Given his physical build, he could have worked his way in at linebacker, or maybe wide receiver. But then the team saw him kick a football for the first time, and here again, everything changed.

Diaz-Nichols first kicked a soccer ball when he was 3 years old. He started playing on a team in soccer-mad Spain when he was 5.

Shortly after meeting his American host family in Thurmont, Diaz-Nichols and one of his host brothers were playing with a football in the backyard. Wanting to show off his strong right leg, Diaz-Nichols booted it over the power lines.

Word quickly traveled around the Catoctin football team. It lined Diaz-Nichols up at the 10-yard line, and he made it. It pushed him back to the 20, and he made it. The 25, and the same thing.

Diaz-Nichols has connected from as far away as 60 yards during practice.

"He is the most naturally gifted kicker I have seen as a coach," said Rich, who was quick to point out that Diaz-Nichols had only been kicking a football for three months. "It comes naturally for him. Once he learns more and more about kicking a football, he'll be kicking 60-yarders in a game."

Diaz-Nichols has made every field goal he has attempted for Catoctin in a game to this point, including one from as far as 40 yards to help win a game at Williamsport on Sept. 14. He has made every extra point to this point.

"It's an absolute weapon," Rich said of Diaz-Nichols' kicking ability. "Any time we get close to the 30 [yard line], we can score points. His knowledge of the game has come so far, too. He knows what to do if there is a bad snap. If the other team has a good return man, I'll ask him to pop the ball up, and he lands [the kickoff] right on the 20. He will put the ball exactly where I asked him to put it."

Initially, the coach and his kicker could barely communicate because of the language barrier. "I had to download Babbel in order to talk to him," Rich said of the language-learning app.

But Diaz-Nichols' English improved substantially in a relatively short period of time. He speaks it well and understands most of what is said to him very quickly. He attributes his rapid improvement to just speaking and hearing the language constantly over an extended duration.

Shortly after joining the team, he was befriended by Merritt.

"The first time I met him, we were walking to practice, and I was making fun of him because he called it training instead of practice," Merritt said.

The two were in the same chemistry class as well, and quickly became close.

"I thought he was funny," Merritt said. "Listening to his accent is so fun."

During lacrosse season, Merritt tore multiple ankle ligaments and required surgery. Normally a defensive lineman for the Cougars' football team, he is sitting out this season to recover and hopes to play next season as a senior.

But the injury meant, "I needed something to do," Merritt said.

Kicking had always intrigued him, and he started working with Diaz-Nichols regularly at practice. Now, during games, Merritt will be wearing a headset and he'll communicate with Rich how Diaz-Nichols is feeling and where he feels comfortable kicking from. He's carved out a valuable role for the team.

Diaz-Nichols, meanwhile, has been blown away by the entire experience. He said that playing football has been "much better" than playing soccer.

"It's a lot of people watching you. It's a lot of emotion. It's great," he said.

He said he was very nervous to come to this country and equally nervous when he attempted his first kick in a game. But he has gradually become more comfortable due to his success and a welcoming environment around him.

He said coming to America has been an "amazing" experience.

"It's like the movies," he said. "In Spain, everyone is similar. Here, you have a lot of different people."

He is scheduled to go back to Spain after the school year, but the team is working on getting his stay extended for another year. And given his vast potential, who knows where that might lead?

His parents get up at 3 a.m. back in Madrid to watch a video stream of Catoctin's games that they project from their iPad onto their TV. His sister can not be bothered to get up, though.

"She is sleeping," Diaz-Nichols said.

On what his parents might be thinking as they watch him play a sport that is still pretty foreign in their land, Diaz-Nichols said, "I think they are proud of me."

— Greg Swatek, The Frederick News-Post
